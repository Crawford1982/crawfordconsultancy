Can an Employee Withdraw Their Resignation?

They handed in their notice, you started planning for their departure — then they asked to stay. Here’s how UK employers can handle withdrawal requests fairly and reduce legal risk.

An employee hands in their notice. You acknowledge their resignation and start making arrangements for them to leave.

A few days later, they change their mind.

They’ve had time to think, they’ve decided they want to stay, and they’re asking if they can withdraw their resignation.

Do you have to let them?

It’s a situation that can leave employers unsure of where they stand — particularly if you’ve already started recruiting a replacement or making changes within the business.

Here’s what you need to know.

Can an employee withdraw their resignation?

Generally, an employee cannot withdraw a clear and valid resignation without their employer’s agreement.

Once an employee has clearly communicated their intention to resign, you do not have to agree to them changing their mind.

However, there are circumstances where things aren’t quite so straightforward.

The way the resignation was given, the employee’s state of mind and how quickly they attempted to withdraw it can all be relevant.

That’s why it’s important to look at the circumstances rather than automatically saying yes or no.

What if they resigned in the heat of the moment?

Imagine an employee has a disagreement with their manager. Things become heated, and the employee says:

“That’s it, I quit!”

They walk out, but later that day — or the following morning — they contact you to say they didn’t mean it.

Can you hold them to their resignation?

Potentially, but you need to be careful.

Where someone resigns during an argument, while particularly distressed or in other unusual circumstances, there may be a genuine question about whether they intended to resign at all.

Simply treating their words as a definite resignation could create legal risks — for example if employment is ended when the employee did not truly intend to leave.

A sensible approach is to give the employee a reasonable opportunity to explain what happened and to confirm whether they genuinely intended to end their employment.

This doesn’t mean every resignation made during an argument is automatically invalid.

It means the circumstances matter.

Does a resignation have to be in writing?

Not necessarily.

An employee can generally resign verbally, although their employment contract may require them to give notice in writing.

From an employer’s perspective, having a written record is always preferable.

If an employee resigns verbally, it’s sensible to ask them to confirm their decision in writing.

You should also acknowledge the resignation, confirming their notice period and expected leaving date.

Clear HR documentation at this stage helps avoid misunderstandings further down the line.

Do employers have to accept a resignation?

This is another common misconception.

An employee doesn’t normally need their employer’s permission to resign.

If they have given a clear and valid resignation, you cannot simply refuse to accept it because you don’t want them to leave.

However, you can have a conversation with them to understand their reasons and explore whether there’s anything that might encourage them to stay.

Similarly, acknowledging a resignation in writing doesn’t automatically settle a dispute about whether the employee genuinely intended to resign in the first place.

What if you’ve already recruited a replacement?

This can make things more complicated.

Perhaps you’ve advertised the position, interviewed candidates or even offered someone the job. Your employee then asks to withdraw their resignation.

If their original resignation was clear and valid, you would not normally be obliged to agree to the withdrawal simply because they’ve changed their mind.

However, if the resignation was made in questionable circumstances, you should be careful about relying solely on the fact that you’ve already started recruitment arrangements.

It’s also worth remembering that a job offer you’ve made to a replacement candidate may create contractual obligations of its own.

What if the employee resigned because of a workplace issue?

This is something I’d encourage employers to explore.

If an employee has resigned following a disagreement, concerns about their treatment or an unresolved workplace issue, it’s worth understanding what’s happened.

  • Have they raised concerns previously?
  • Do they feel they’ve been treated unfairly?
  • Was their resignation a reaction to something that happened at work?

An employee who feels forced to resign because of a serious breach of their employment contract may potentially have grounds for a constructive dismissal claim.

So, before simply accepting that they’re leaving, consider whether there’s an underlying issue that needs attention.

What should you do if an employee asks to withdraw their resignation?

You don’t necessarily need a formal HR process, but you should handle the request carefully.

  1. Establish what happened — look at how the resignation was communicated and the circumstances surrounding it.
  2. Speak to the employee — find out why they resigned and why they now want to withdraw it.
  3. Consider whether the resignation was genuinely intended — particularly where emotions, health concerns or other unusual circumstances may have influenced the decision.
  4. Consider the business circumstances — including any arrangements already made, such as recruitment or changes to staffing.
  5. Confirm your decision in writing — whether you agree to the withdrawal or not, make sure the outcome is clear and documented.

If you agree to the withdrawal, confirm that the resignation has been withdrawn by agreement and that employment will continue on the same terms (unless you have agreed something different).

If you don’t agree, make sure you’re confident the original resignation was valid and that you’ve properly considered the circumstances.

Sometimes a conversation is all that’s needed

Not every employee who resigns wants to leave because they dislike their job.

Sometimes they’re frustrated. Sometimes they’ve had a disagreement. And sometimes they make a decision they later regret.

Having a conversation doesn’t mean you have to persuade them to stay or agree to withdraw their resignation.

But it can help you understand what’s really going on and avoid making assumptions.

For small businesses, where one employee leaving can have a significant impact, taking a little time to establish the facts can be worthwhile.

The important thing is not to rush your decision

If an employee has clearly and genuinely resigned, you generally don’t have to allow them to withdraw it.

But if there is any doubt about whether they really intended to resign — particularly where the decision was made in the heat of the moment — it’s worth taking advice before treating their employment as ended.

A situation that could have been resolved with a conversation can become much more complicated if it’s handled incorrectly.
