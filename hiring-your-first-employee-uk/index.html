Hiring Your First Employee? Everything You Need Before Day One

Taking on your first employee is an exciting milestone — and it can feel daunting. Here’s a straightforward UK checklist for small businesses getting ready to hire.

Taking on your first employee usually means your business is growing, you’re getting busier, and you need an extra pair of hands. Many business owners worry about getting things wrong, especially when it comes to employment law, payroll and paperwork. The good news is that with a little preparation, the process doesn’t need to be complicated.

Here’s a straightforward guide to what you should have in place before your new employee starts — from planning the role to contracts, payroll and their first day.

1. Decide what you actually need

Before advertising a role, take a step back and think about what your business really needs.

Ask yourself:

  • What tasks are taking up most of my time?
  • Is this a permanent or temporary role?
  • Do I need someone full-time or part-time?
  • What skills are essential?
  • Could this role grow as my business grows?

Taking time to plan now can save you time, money and recruitment headaches later. It’s also worth budgeting for the full cost of employment — not just the salary — as explained in our guide to how much it really costs to employ someone in the UK.

2. Register as an employer

Before you can pay an employee, you’ll usually need to register as an employer with HMRC.

You’ll also need Employers’ Liability Insurance, which is a legal requirement for most businesses that employ staff.

Getting these essentials in place before your employee starts means everything is ready from day one.

3. Carry out Right to Work checks

Every employer in the UK has a legal responsibility to check that anyone they employ has the right to work in the UK.

These checks must be completed before employment begins, and you’ll need to keep copies of the documents you’ve checked.

Failing to carry out the correct checks can result in significant penalties, so it’s important not to overlook this step.

4. Issue a written employment contract

Every employee should receive a written statement of employment particulars from day one.

A well-written contract should clearly explain things like:

  • Job title
  • Hours of work
  • Salary
  • Holiday entitlement
  • Place of work
  • Notice periods
  • Probation period
  • Benefits (where applicable)

Having everything in writing helps protect both you and your employee and sets clear expectations from the beginning. Crawford Consultancy can help with contracts as part of our HR support.

5. Set up payroll

Paying wages involves much more than simply transferring money into someone’s bank account.

You’ll need to:

  • Register your employee with HMRC
  • Collect the information you need to run payroll
  • Calculate tax and National Insurance correctly
  • Provide payslips
  • Submit payroll information to HMRC on time
  • Keep accurate payroll records

Many small businesses choose to outsource payroll so they can focus on running their business while knowing everything is being dealt with correctly.

6. Understand your workplace pension duties

Every employer has responsibilities under workplace pension legislation.

Whether your employee qualifies for automatic enrolment or not, you’ll still have duties to assess your workforce and comply with pension requirements.

Understanding your responsibilities from the beginning helps avoid problems later.

7. Put your policies in place

While not every policy is legally required, having clear guidance for employees helps prevent misunderstandings and creates consistency as your business grows.

An employee handbook might include:

  • Annual leave
  • Sickness absence
  • Family leave
  • Disciplinary and grievance procedures
  • Social media
  • Standards of conduct

Introducing these documents early gives everyone clear expectations from day one.

8. Plan a great first day

A good induction helps your new employee settle in quickly and gives them confidence from the moment they join.

Think about:

  • Introducing colleagues
  • Health and safety information
  • Training
  • Company values
  • Systems and processes
  • Setting expectations

A positive first impression often leads to a more engaged and productive employee.

9. Keep your employee records organised

Once your employee has started, it’s important to keep accurate records throughout their employment. Not only does this help you stay organised, but it also demonstrates compliance if you’re ever asked to produce information.

As a minimum, you should keep records such as:

  • Employment contract and any changes to terms and conditions
  • Right to Work documents
  • Emergency contact details
  • HMRC starter information
  • Payroll records and payslips
  • Workplace pension records
  • Annual leave entitlement and holiday taken
  • Sickness absence records
  • Training completed and any relevant qualifications
  • Performance reviews or probation notes (where applicable)

Keeping everything organised from the start will save time and make managing your employee much easier as your business grows.

Final thoughts

Hiring your first employee is a huge milestone and an exciting sign that your business is growing.

While there are legal responsibilities to consider, getting the right foundations in place from the beginning will make life much easier — for both you and your employee.

If you’re unsure where to start, getting support early can save you time, money and unnecessary stress.
