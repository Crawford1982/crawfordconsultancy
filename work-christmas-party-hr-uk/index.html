The Work Christmas Party: Fun, Festivities and a Few HR Things Worth Remembering!

It’s nearly party season. Here’s how UK small employers can celebrate with their team while managing harassment, misconduct and inclusivity risks — without taking the fun out of the night.

It’s nearly that time of year again.

Christmas nights out are being organised, restaurants are getting booked and everyone’s looking forward to a chance to let their hair down after another busy year.

And quite rightly too.

A Christmas night out is a lovely way to thank your team, bring everyone together and celebrate the year.

But from an employer’s point of view, there are a few things worth thinking about before the celebrations begin.

Because while everyone might be off the clock, that doesn’t necessarily mean your responsibilities as an employer have ended.

Short answer

A staff Christmas party can still be a work-related event. Plan ahead on behaviour expectations, harassment prevention, inclusivity and how you’ll handle any complaint that lands on your desk in January.

Is the Christmas party really an extension of the workplace?

Potentially, yes.

A Christmas party organised by the business can be considered a work-related event, even if it takes place in a restaurant, hotel or pub outside normal working hours.

That means employers could still find themselves dealing with complaints about inappropriate behaviour, harassment or misconduct.

That doesn’t mean you’re automatically responsible for everything an employee does after a few drinks. Whether the business has legal responsibility will depend on the circumstances, including how closely the incident is connected to employment.

But it does mean that dismissing a complaint with “Well, it happened outside work” isn’t necessarily going to be enough.

Tribunals have considered conduct at work social events before. The detail always matters, but the message for employers is simple: treat a serious complaint seriously, even if it happened after hours.

A few drinks are fine. An excuse for poor behaviour? Not so much.

We’ve probably all heard stories about Christmas nights out that didn’t quite go to plan.

Someone has one too many, an argument breaks out, or a comment is made that someone else finds upsetting.

While you can’t control every conversation or interaction, you can take sensible steps to reduce the likelihood of problems. For example:

  • Remind employees that the usual standards of respectful behaviour still apply.
  • Make sure managers set a good example.
  • Consider the risks associated with alcohol, particularly if the business is paying for drinks.
  • Ensure employees know who they can speak to if something makes them uncomfortable.
  • Think about safe travel home and whether late finishes create avoidable risks.

You don’t need to turn the Christmas party into a formal work event with a list of rules pinned to the wall.

Sometimes a simple, friendly reminder beforehand is enough to reinforce expectations — alongside the other preventative measures appropriate for your business.

If your policies cover misconduct, harassment and dignity at work, they should still be relevant. The point is to make sure people know that before the prosecco starts flowing.

Harassment is something employers need to take seriously

This is particularly important following changes to employment legislation.

Since 26 October 2024, employers in Great Britain have had a legal duty to take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their workers. That preventative duty can apply to work-related social events, not just day-to-day work on site.

From 30 October 2026, that duty is strengthened to require employers to take all reasonable steps. Additional protections concerning harassment by third parties, such as customers and clients, also come into force — see our guide on employer liability for customer harassment for more detail.

Harassment under the Equality Act 2010 can include unwanted conduct related to a protected characteristic, or sexual harassment, where it has the purpose or effect of violating dignity or creating an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment.

So, what does this mean in practice for a Christmas night out?

Employers should think ahead about potential risks and take appropriate steps to prevent harassment, rather than simply reacting if something goes wrong.

That might include considering alcohol, the venue, transport arrangements, interactions with third parties at shared venues and how employees can raise concerns.

The steps required will depend on the circumstances of your business and the event.

Having a policy is important, but a policy sitting in a folder that nobody has read isn’t enough on its own. Training, reminders and manager behaviour often matter just as much.

What if two employees have an argument?

Imagine two colleagues have an argument at the Christmas party.

Things get heated, a few unpleasant comments are exchanged and, by Monday morning, neither wants to work with the other.

Is that now your problem as the employer?

Quite possibly.

Even where an incident happens outside the normal workplace, employers may need to address it if it affects working relationships, the business or the wellbeing of employees.

The first step is to establish what actually happened.

Depending on the seriousness of the incident, this might involve informal conversations, mediation or a formal investigation.

If disciplinary action is being considered, employers should follow a fair process and the relevant Acas guidance.

It’s important not to jump straight to conclusions based on rumours or one person’s account of the evening.

And remember, just because alcohol was involved doesn’t mean inappropriate behaviour should automatically be excused.

What about photos and social media?

Another thing worth considering is what happens when the photographs start appearing online.

A few innocent pictures of everyone enjoying themselves are unlikely to cause any problems.

But what if someone posts an embarrassing photograph of a colleague without their permission? Or shares a video that reflects badly on the business?

Employees should be mindful of what they share, particularly where colleagues or the business can be identified.

It’s worth reminding everyone to respect other people’s privacy and think before posting.

And if something inappropriate does appear online, consider the circumstances and any relevant policies before deciding what action, if any, is necessary.

Make sure everyone feels included

Not everyone enjoys a big night out, and not everyone drinks alcohol.

Some employees might have family commitments, religious considerations, disabilities or simply prefer a quieter evening.

Where possible, try to organise something that gives everyone a fair opportunity to take part.

Make attendance voluntary, offer non-alcoholic drinks and think about accessibility and transport arrangements.

After all, the purpose is to thank your employees and bring people together — not make anyone feel uncomfortable or left out.

A little planning goes a long way

Before your Christmas celebrations, it’s worth asking yourself:

  • Have we considered any obvious risks associated with the event?
  • Do employees understand the standards of behaviour expected?
  • Have we taken appropriate steps to prevent harassment?
  • Is there someone employees can approach if a problem arises?
  • Are the arrangements inclusive and accessible?
  • Do we know how we’d handle a complaint afterwards?

None of this needs to take the fun out of the evening.

It’s simply about making sure everyone can enjoy themselves safely and respectfully.

Enjoy the celebrations!

Christmas nights out should be something employees look forward to.

They’re a chance to celebrate achievements, enjoy each other’s company and finish the year on a positive note.

And with a little thought beforehand, there’s no reason they shouldn’t be exactly that.

Because the last thing any employer wants is for a great Christmas night out to become January’s HR headache.
