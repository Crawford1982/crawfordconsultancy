Could Your Business Run Without You for a Week?

If essential knowledge lives in one person’s head, your business is more fragile than it looks. Here’s how to document what matters and build a more resilient way of working.

If you unexpectedly couldn’t work tomorrow, would someone else know what needed to be done?

Would they know which suppliers need paying? When payroll needs processed? Which customers need contacted? Where important documents are stored? Or which tasks absolutely have to happen before the end of the week?

This applies whether you’re the business owner or the person everyone else relies on — the administrator, payroll contact or manager who holds the key information.

The goal isn’t to remove yourself from the business. It’s to make sure the business doesn’t stop if you’re unavailable.

For many small businesses, a surprising amount of essential information sits with one person — often the business owner. That’s understandable when you’re starting out. You know how everything works because you’ve built the business yourself. But as the business grows, relying on one person’s knowledge can quickly become a risk.

The “it’s all in my head” problem

Small businesses don’t usually set out to have poor processes. They develop naturally.

You start doing something one way. It works, so you keep doing it. A spreadsheet is created. A folder appears on someone’s desktop. A reminder goes into a diary.

Before long, you have dozens of little processes keeping the business running — but very few of them are actually written down.

That’s fine while the person who knows the process is available. The problem comes when they’re not.

And this doesn’t only apply to business owners. You may have an administrator who is the only person who knows how a particular system works, a payroll contact who knows all the important deadlines, or a manager who has key supplier information stored in their own inbox.

That’s when a person becomes a single point of failure for the business.

Start with your essential business tasks

You don’t need a huge corporate operations manual. Start by identifying the things that simply have to happen for your business to continue operating.

Depending on your business, that might include:

  • Payroll and employee payments
  • Invoicing and credit control
  • Paying suppliers
  • Processing customer orders
  • Responding to important customer enquiries
  • Ordering stock or materials
  • Completing statutory or regulatory submissions
  • Managing employee holidays and absence
  • Renewing licences, insurance or subscriptions
  • Regular reporting
  • Key customer or supplier communications

Now ask yourself: Who knows how to do each of these things?

If the answer is only one person, that’s an area worth looking at.

Write down how things are actually done

Documenting a process doesn’t have to mean producing a ten-page procedure. For many routine tasks, a simple step-by-step guide is enough.

For example:

Monthly invoicing

1. Where is the information taken from?

2. Who prepares the invoices?

3. Who checks or approves them?

4. How are they issued?

5. Where are copies stored?

6. What happens if an invoice isn’t paid?

The aim is to make the process clear enough that another appropriate person could understand what needs to happen.

Screenshots, checklists and simple flowcharts can sometimes be much more useful than pages of written instructions.

Make sure important information is accessible

There’s little benefit in documenting your processes if nobody can find them.

Think about where your business stores:

  • Policies and procedures
  • Templates
  • Supplier information
  • Customer records
  • Contracts
  • Employee documentation
  • Finance and payroll information
  • System instructions
  • Key dates and deadlines

Try to create a logical structure rather than having information scattered across individual inboxes, desktops, paper files and multiple versions of the same spreadsheet.

Access should, of course, still be appropriate. Sensitive employee, payroll, financial and customer information shouldn’t simply be available to everyone. The goal is controlled access, not unrestricted access.

Don’t forget passwords and system access

This is an area businesses can easily overlook.

If the only person with access to an essential system — payroll software, online banking, HMRC or your customer database — is suddenly unavailable, how would the business continue?

That doesn’t mean sharing passwords between employees. Instead, consider whether your systems have appropriate administrator access, whether more than one authorised person can access essential services, and whether recovery information is kept securely and kept up to date.

It’s much easier to resolve this now than when someone is unexpectedly unavailable.

Look at your deadlines

Some business tasks can wait a few days. Others can’t.

Payroll deadlines, tax submissions, customer commitments, licence renewals and supplier payments can all have consequences if they’re missed.

A simple shared calendar or recurring task list can make a significant difference. Record:

  • What needs to happen
  • When it needs to happen
  • Who is responsible
  • Who can cover it
  • Where the instructions or information are stored

You’re essentially removing the need for someone to remember everything.

Cross-train where it makes sense

Documentation is useful, but sometimes another person genuinely needs to know how to perform the task.

Consider which activities are critical enough to require cover. If only one employee knows how to run payroll, process orders or complete an essential monthly report, what happens when they’re on annual leave?

Even basic cross-training can reduce that dependency. It can also make holidays considerably less stressful for the person who normally carries the responsibility.

Review the processes that don’t make sense anymore

There’s another benefit to writing down how your business operates: you start noticing things.

Why are we entering the same information into three different spreadsheets? Why does this need four approvals? Why do we print this, sign it and then scan it back in? Why are we still doing this manually?

Processes often remain in place simply because that’s how they’ve always been done. Documenting them gives you an opportunity to question whether there’s a quicker, simpler or more reliable way.

You don’t necessarily need expensive new software either. Sometimes a clearer process, better spreadsheet, shared template or simple change in responsibility can solve the problem.

Test it

Once you’ve documented your key processes, there’s a simple way to find the gaps.

Take some time away. You don’t necessarily need to disappear for a week. Let someone else follow one of your documented processes while you’re available to answer questions.

Every question they need to ask is potentially something that could be clearer. Then update the process.

Over time, you create a business that is much less dependent on individual knowledge.

Could your business run without you?

For a small business owner, being needed can sometimes feel like a sign that things are going well. But there’s a difference between being valuable to your business and being essential to every single task within it.

Good processes don’t remove your importance. They give you more time to focus on the things that actually need your expertise, while making the day-to-day running of the business more consistent and resilient.

So, ask yourself: if I couldn’t work for the next seven days, what would stop?

Whatever comes to mind first is probably a good place to start.
